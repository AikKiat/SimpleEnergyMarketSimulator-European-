From my research, this is the part that makes electricity markets a *strategic game* and not just an engineering problem: a plant may offer **any price it likes** — honesty is not enforced. Offering above your true marginal cost means pocketing the difference **if you still clear**; offering too high means clearing nothing and earning zero.

Overall, there are 2 forms of **withholding** that caught my eye:

- **Economic withholding**: As the aforementioned - raising ask prices above real objective marginal costs. Thus, submitting strategic bids that deviate from the true marginal cost or utility.
- **Physical withholding**: Intentionally throttling generation output to drive up price.

(Anderson, 2025)

More broadly, "Gaming in the context of electricity markets refers to situations when participants manipulate rules or scenarios to gain unfair advantages" (Zaidi, 2025), with producers using "various tactics to manipulate these complex systems such as creating artificial congestion on transmission lines, strategically withholding generation capacity or submitting misleading cost information" (Zaidi, 2025).

A key example I found was the **2000–2001 California Electricity Crisis**: (physical and economic withholding)

- A company by the name of **Enron** purposefully shut down power plants or conducted maintenance during days of peak demand.
- Enron's traders also ran **congestion-scheduling schemes** (with internal names like "Death Star") on the single constrained connection line between the northern and southern power grids of California, profiting from the artificial scarcity — allowing it to price-gouge and sell electricity at super high prices due to the drastic drop in supply amidst high demand.
- Back then, since government price caps were placed on the retail price of electricity, downstream entities such as electricity retailers **Pacific Gas and Electric Company (PG&E)** as well as **Southern California Edison** had to buy wholesale electricity at super high prices and sell at the regulated, incurring huge losses.
- Eventually, PG&E suffered **bankruptcy** while Southern California Edison came close to folding as well.

("2000–2001 California electricity crisis," n.d.)

On the scale of it: "Enron, along with several other energy trading companies, exploited fundamental flaws in California's newly deregulated electricity market structure" and "Electricity prices surged by an astonishing **800%** over just eight months" (Zaidi, 2025).

As such, in the EU strong regulation and bidding conduct is enforced under **REMIT** (Regulation on Wholesale Energy Market Integrity and Transparency). It is "the EU framework that protects wholesale energy markets from abuse, ensuring open and fair competition", prohibiting **insider trading** — "using undisclosed information to gain unfair advantage" — and **market manipulation** — "distorting market integrity or creating false or misleading market signals" (ACER, n.d.).

Capacity withholding and manipulative bidding draw real fines — trading houses run compliance functions precisely because this temptation exists.

**We can see this in action for this simulated example.** The modern CCGT's honest cost is around £65/MWh. However, we can push up the offer and exercise economic withholding.

To conclude, we can see that strategic bidding is a key component of competition within the market. However, it must also be put under strict regulation and should not deviate from organisations' ultimate missions to supply the right sufficient energy quotas that power the electrical grids we rely on every single day.
