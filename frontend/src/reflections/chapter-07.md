"For much of the last century...environmental pollution has been considered a negative externality." (David et al., 2026)

"Conventional combustion plants contribute to secondary
particulate matter formation through atmospheric
reactions involving NOx and SO2. Units firing hard coal
and lignite, as well as biomass, also emit fine particles
directly, resulting in primary particulate emissions" (RWE, 2025)

Therefore, carbon pricing has been a key implementation strategy to reduce environmental pollution. In the theoretical sense it leverages on several market mechanisms such as the determination of the merit order and clearing price, to steer the market and overall future capital expenditure towards cleaner, greener solutions.

So how does it work? Based on my findings, "the policy options...for implementing a carbon price are defined...as follows:

- Cap-and-trade: A cap-and-trade system places a limit (the cap) on emissions within a defined area of economic activity. Firms that wish to emit carbon through their activities are required to buy permits to cover these emissions, and the supply of these permits is limited by the cap. Firms can trade permits, and the carbon price emerges from this market. The cap is usually, though not always, set to decrease over time.

- Tax: A tax requires firms within a defined area of economic activity to pay a fixed price for each tonne of carbon they emit. This price can be kept level or changed over time by governments."

(David et al., 2026, *A Dynamic Comparison of the Cost-Effectiveness of Carbon Pricing Policies*)

Rudimentarily, the cost of factoring carbon generation is seen as :

`marginal cost = (fuel price ÷ efficiency) + (tonnes CO₂ per MWh × carbon price)`

And it represents the internalisation of this negative externality. Alluding to the above 2 options of how carbon pricing can be factored in:
- Carbon tax - the much simpler implementation where governments implement a tax of x€/tonne of Co2 produced, and this is immediately considered into the equation.

- Cap and Trade, like the aforementioned explanation, forces firms to compete for a limited set of permits to cover their emissions, and the cap shrinks over time.

>"Each year, the falling emissions cap results in a decreasing amount of permits being available for companies to buy. All emissions not covered by permits incur a fine of £100 per tonne of CO2. This scenario begins to affect companies from tick 1 (i.e., year 2)...At the beginning of the second year, the ETS cap is set, and from the following year, it is reduced on a linear trajectory whose gradient can be (adjusted)..." (David et al., 2026)

`Note that the £100 fine above is a parameter of the authors' model. In the real EU ETS, the excess emissions penalty was set at €100/tonne and is indexed to inflation — and crucially, paying the penalty does not cancel the obligation: the firm must still surrender the missing allowances on top of the fine (European Commission, 2026).`

In the EU ETS for example, it enforces a €100/tonne excess emissions penalty for not obtaining permits (European Commission, 2026).

As a result, when a fossil plant for instance becomes the marginal plant, the carbon cost is also from the above - culminated inside its offer to be the clearing price. Carbon costs flow downstream, also benefiting cleaner generators... they receive the carbon-inflated clearing price while bearing none of the cost, and that widened margin is precisely the investment signal.

Overall, from all of this we can see the economic elegance of the carbon pricing strategy. It exercises regulation through mechanisms that embody the core of a free, competitive market. It also builds upon the existing foundations of the energy power market, such as merit order and the overall algorithmic loop process of the Day Ahead Auction to calculate clearing prices - building on these abstractions to impose a clean method of ingesting externalities; inculcating a more ethically responsible private market. Ultimately with such a market that supports cleaner energy price signals, this consensus also empowers existing companies' ambitions towards embracing renewables and de-carbonisation - fuelling the energy transition.

>"As digitalisation, electrification, and industrial decarbonisation accelerate, the demand for affordable, secure, and clean electricity will grow substantially. This makes the shift to renewable energy, flexible generation, and storage technologies not only an environmental imperative but also a societal and economic one..to achieve Net Zero emissions by 2040...certified by the Science Based Targets initiative and support the 1.5°C target of the Paris Agreement." (RWE, 2026)

(European Commission, 2026)
