"In markets with high renewable penetration, situations increasingly arise in which electricity supply exceeds demand — and the market price falls below zero." (Montel, 2025) From my exploration, European **day-ahead** prices have now gone **negative** for hundreds of hours per year in Germany alone:

>"573 out of a total of 8,760 hours in 2025, compared with 457 out of 8,784 hours in 2024. Prices above €300/MWh were recorded in 40 out of 8,760 hours in 2025, compared with 41 out of 8,784 hours in 2024. (The total number of hours in the two years is different because 2024 was a leap year.)" (Bundesnetzagentur, 2026)

Being *paid to consume* is not a very conventional, intuitive idea. However, here are some explanations I found and the overall phenomenon is actually a clear sign of the unequivocal transition towards renewable energy sources. As we hearken back to the previous chapters and even the grounding of the power market - Futures, Day Ahead, intraday and even the market agents (**Balance Responsible Parties**) who seek to balance either their own portfolios, or the TSOs come in after the transactions to ensure market equilibrium; all of these can be traced back to the concept that electricity **cannot be stored**, or at least storing it is **very costly and expensive**.

> "Negative prices occur when generators are willing to pay the market to take electricity. In central and eastern Europe (CEE), this happens during hours when supply overwhelms demand and the grid cannot export or absorb the surplus. The negative power prices CEE story is not a market failure; it's a price signal. It tells generators to reduce output, nudges flexible demand to switch on, and highlights where the network is congested or lacks storage." (Montel, 2025)

> "The drivers of negative electricity prices in the Netherlands can be grouped into three dimensions—**structural**, **institutional**, and **technical**....

>"Structural reasons (dominant): Rapid growth in installed PV has markedly increased variable renewable output...resulting in periodic supply surpluses."

>"Institutional reasons: Market rules permit negative-prices bids, and certain design features incentivize such bidding. Accordingly renewable generators and conventional thermal generators have adopted the following distinct approaches."

> "The Renewable Energy Grant Scheme (SDE+), introduced in 2016, provides a premium subsidy (initially €150/MWh), enabling renewable generators to remain profitable during brief intervals of negative prices...(since) European electricity markets are typically decentralized and often lack...cost compensation guarantees...for thermal units; (they) owing to technical and cost constraints, (face) intense market competition and extremely low prices - ...compelled to submit negative bids to secure dispatch and avoid incurring even higher costs associated with shutting down and restarting."

(Sun et al., 2026)

Therefore, from all this we can see the key explanation for the ongoing trend of negative prices, and how it is in fact a natural causal phenomenon that follows from how renewable energy plants do not incur any **marginal costs** for operating. All these coupled with state **subsidies** to encourage shifting energy generation to renewable sources, has paved the way for renewables to be fitted with ask prices that can go below 0, as far as the subsidy amount subtracts beyond the original marginal cost which is as aforementioned - 0 euros. As such, this over-extension below the price floor enacts great swings in the market price at equilibrium to negative levels. Legacy conventional plants such as CCGTs which do in fact need fossil fuels to generate electricity, are thus edged out of the market. They are forced to choose between undertaking losses by bidding along with the negative, or incurring operational costs of the wasted initial startup by ceasing operations / shutting down.

Overall, this situation is also made more profound due to the unprecedented nature of power generation through renewables - in that supply is tied to physical environmental conditions and can thus vary over a broad range:

> "Negative pricing tends to cluster at certain times of day and seasons because the same conditions repeat:

>"Wind surges: Strong wind systems crossing Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary and Romania can lift output steeply during night and early-morning hours when demand is low. Onshore fleets are growing fast, so the amplitude of these ramps increases year over year.

>"Hydro and run-of-river flows: Alpine and Carpathian catchments can flood the system with hydro in spring snowmelt or during wet weeks. Operators may bid low (even negative) to prioritise reservoir management, avoid spillage, or meet environmental flow constraints."

(Montel, 2025)

Looking at all this, I too visualised my own example:

On a windy, sunny, low-demand afternoon there is a lot of generation for a wind farm, which is also subsidised with £50/MWh in support *per MWh generated*.

Looking at this, we can see that the rational break-even offer can be less than £0 since the subsidy is earned only on electricity actually generated — if the wind farm stops, it forfeits that £50/MWh entirely. It is therefore still better off running at any market price above **−£50**, and so a rational extrapolation of its offer would be **−£50**.

Assuming this trend of ask prices continues (less than £0 due to large proportion of renewables within the market as part of the energy transition) the clearing price can possibly be −£40, which is also the **uniform clearing price** (chapter 4). The market price therefore clears below zero. A natural consequence on legacy CCGTs would be as mentioned above - incur losses and/or be edged out of the market.

All in all, I feel that this is in fact a **necessary** and **natural** reality that will occur given the need to embrace more renewable sources of energy. Throughout history it has been proven that relying on fossil fuels, although a process that has championed the world's energy supply for almost a century, is ultimately of eventual detriment to our environment. As such, alluding to my previous findings about the carbon tax, **EU ETS** and notable goals to "achieve Net Zero emissions by 2040" (RWE, 2026), we can see that this is a real economic effect that justifies - same as the carbon pricing formula - a need for a change in market mindsets and signals, a redirection of capital towards more renewable sources to engender a more sustainable world through an ethically responsible free **energy market**.
